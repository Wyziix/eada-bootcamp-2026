Lesson 01 / The big idea · 3 min

Meet your data.
Before you
model it.

A spreadsheet is full of numbers. EDA helps you turn them into questions, patterns, and a sensible next step.

REAL DATA. SMALL STEPS. ZERO CODE.
Ames, Iowa2,930 home sales

EDA stands for exploratory data analysis. It means inspecting, summarizing, and visualizing data to understand what is there, spot problems, and develop questions before drawing firm conclusions.

Think like a detective.

A loop, not a checklist
01 / ASKStart with curiosityWhat does a typical home sell for?
02 / LOOKMake it visibleSummarize prices. Draw a chart.
03 / QUESTIONNotice the unexpectedWhy are a few homes so expensive?
04 / RECORDKeep the evidenceWrite what you see, and what to check.

EDA can suggest cleaning decisions and modeling ideas. Cleaning changes the data; modeling learns a rule for prediction. Exploring comes first, and you may return to it many times.

Different variables need different questions.

Numeric

Measurements or counts. Living area is measured; bathrooms are counted. Ask about range, typical value, and spread.

Categorical

Groups or labels, such as neighborhood. Count the homes in each group. Some categories, such as quality ratings, have an order.

Identifiers

A parcel ID identifies a property. Its digits are a label; their average has no useful meaning. A number is not always a measurement.

Read the center and the spread

The median is the middle value after sorting. The mean is the arithmetic average. Neither tells you everything: two datasets can have the same center and very different spreads.

Choose a chart that fits

Use a histogram for the distribution of numeric values. A box plot summarizes the median, middle 50%, and possible outliers. For categories such as neighborhood, use a bar chart of counts.

Lesson 04 / Relationships · 4 min

Now, connect the dots.

Looking at two variables is called bivariate analysis. Does sale price vary with living area? Does it differ across neighborhoods?

Larger homes tend to sell for more

All 2,930 sales · one dot = one sale

Numeric × numeric → scatter plot

Look for direction, strength, curves, clusters, and unusual points. Pearson’s correlation (r) summarizes a linear relationship from −1 to +1; a value near zero can still hide a nonlinear pattern.

Category × numeric → compare groups

Switch to neighborhood to compare group medians. Always check group sizes and variation. A median hides the range of prices within each neighborhood.

Association is a clue, not a cause. Larger homes may also differ in quality, location, or age. The scatter plot alone does not show what would happen if you enlarged a particular house. A correlation is also not a model accuracy score.

Lesson 05 / Data quality · 3 min

Be curious.
Be a little skeptical.

A strange value might be an error, an unusual real home, or a code you haven’t understood yet. Investigate before changing it.

  1. Missing values: unknown is not always absent.

    The starter data has one blank garage-capacity value. That means unknown, not automatically zero. In the full data dictionary, NA in Pool QC means “No Pool.” Those are different meanings.

  2. Duplicates: repeated rows need context.

    Check exact duplicate records and repeated property IDs. A repeat could be an accidental copy or a legitimate later sale. Check dates and record definitions before removing it.

  3. Outliers: unusual does not mean wrong.

    Five homes have more than 4,000 sq ft of above-ground living area. Flag them and inspect the original records, sale conditions, and other features. Their size alone is not a reason to delete them.

  4. Consistency: use simple reality checks.

    Check units, allowed categories, and dates. Two garage construction years are later than the recorded sale year. That is a review flag; check the source before choosing a correction.

Keep an audit trail. Record the issue, your evidence, your decision, and any effect on the analysis. Keep the original data. This course visualizes the observations without filling blanks or removing rows.

Lesson 06 / Your turn · 3 min

Turn a chart
into a useful note.

The output of EDA is understanding. A good note separates what you observed, what you cannot conclude, and what you will check next.

An example worth keeping

Observation. In these 2,930 historical sales, the median price is $160,000 and the mean is about $180,796. The histogram has a long right tail.

Limit. These are Ames sales from 2006–2010. They do not establish today’s prices or represent every housing market.

Next step. Inspect the expensive sales and compare price distributions within relevant groups. Document any later transformation.

Write your first EDA note.

Use the living-area chart, the neighborhood comparison, or the price histogram. Keep claims specific to the observed data.

Observation: Living area and price have a positive association, with substantial variation in price at similar sizes.

Limit: This does not prove that increasing living area causes a particular price increase.

Next step: Compare similarly sized homes by quality and neighborhood, and review the five homes above 4,000 sq ft.

Before predictive modeling: protect the final test set. This course uses the full dataset to teach exploration. For a fair model evaluation, reserve an untouched holdout before data-driven feature choices or tuning. Because you have already explored these rows, use a genuinely unseen evaluation dataset for a strict final test. Learn imputation and encoding rules from training data only.

Your pocket checklist.

  • Define the question and what a row represents.
  • Check types, units, and the dictionary.
  • Look at distributions, centers, and spread.
  • Compare variables and inspect group sizes.
  • Investigate missing, repeated, and unusual values.
  • Write a finding, a limit, and a next step.
Optional: what this looks like in Python

For the starter CSV, these pandas commands are a first look. Use the full data dictionary before parsing the full CSV, where literal NA values can be meaningful categories.

import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("dat/ames/ames_starter.csv")
df.head()                       # Preview records
df.info()                       # Types and non-null counts
df.describe()                   # Numeric summaries
df.isna().sum()                 # Blank / missing counts
df["neighborhood"].value_counts() # Category frequencies
df["sale_price_usd"].hist()      # Price distribution
df.plot.scatter(x="living_area_sqft", y="sale_price_usd")
